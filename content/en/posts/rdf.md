People are often amazed by leaders like Steve Jobs and Elon Musk, who seem able to bend reality to their will. There's even a phrase for it: the Reality Distortion Field (RDF). I want to understand what makes this approach work, and why it works for them when it fails for others.

Jobs was famous for walking into a room of skeptical engineers and walking out with a team convinced it could do something it had considered impossible. Take the early days of the Macintosh, which took a long time to boot. Andy Hertzfeld tells the story of Jobs asking Larry Kenyon, the engineer working on the disk driver, whether he would find a way to shave ten seconds off the boot time if it could save a person's life. Kenyon allowed that he probably could, and a few weeks later he had cut it by 28 seconds.

Musk does something similar at SpaceX and Tesla. When an engineer says rockets can't be reused, or that batteries can't be made cheaply enough for mass-market electric cars, Musk asks why not, and what's stopping us. Behind those questions is a line he often repeats: "Physics is the law, everything else is a recommendation." He separates two kinds of limits: the ones physics sets, and assumptions that people have come to treat as laws.

What Jobs and Musk are both doing is what philosophers call Socratic questioning, and people often misunderstand how it works. Repeating "Why not?" isn't enough. Asked without real curiosity and a shared purpose, the question quickly becomes annoying (it sounds like a boss being difficult), or worse, insulting to the very experts whose cooperation you need.

The questions need a mission behind them. A mission gives them context and direction, so a question that could sound annoying becomes logical and necessary. Jobs wasn't pushing for a faster boot time to torment his engineers; every question tied back to the mission of making "computers for the rest of us." Musk doesn't insist on reusable rockets to frustrate his engineers either; he does it because the mission is to get humanity to Mars.

It also takes real curiosity, a beginner's mindset. You ask because you want to understand, not to prove you're smarter. Jobs often asked about technical details because he wanted to understand them himself, and Musk spends a lot of time digging into the physics and engineering problems alongside his teams.

The best questions also lead the expert to a breakthrough that feels like their own idea. People are far more committed to ideas they believe they came up with themselves.

Of course, questions alone aren't enough. The team has to know that the questions aren't idle or academic. Jobs backed his with action, and people could see him putting resources into prototypes. Musk puts enormous personal risk and capital behind his.
