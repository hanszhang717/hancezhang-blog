Most people hear "seek truth from facts" and think it means "be honest." That is the most harmless, yet useless reading of the phrase. When Mao Zedong inscribed it as the motto of the Party School in Yan'an, he was declaring war on a specific enemy: dogmatism, the delusion that ideas should dominate reality rather than serve it. In his 1941 essay *Reform Our Study*, he took the phrase apart like this:

> "'Facts' are all the things that exist objectively, 'truth' means their internal relations, that is, the laws governing them, and 'to seek' means to study."

The "seeking" here means actively doing and investigating; watching from the sidelines isn't enough. Earlier, in his 1937 essay *On Practice*, he wrote:

> "Knowledge begins with practice, and theoretical knowledge is acquired through practice and must then return to practice... The active function of knowledge manifests itself not only in the active leap from perceptual to rational knowledge, but also, and more importantly, it must be manifested in the leap from rational knowledge to revolutionary practice."

Strip away the political vocabulary, and what remains is a cold rule about knowledge: an idea that reality hasn't tested is worth nothing, and when reality and your ideas collide, reality always wins. "Seek truth from facts" has little to do with honesty. What it asks is whether you're willing to let reality rewrite your beliefs, continuously and without mercy.

## The Map Is Not The Territory

Most people never really face this question: should your values serve reality, or should reality bend to fit your values? We inherit a set of values from school, family, and society, such as "Study hard and you'll get a good job," "Be loyal and the company will take care of you," and "Follow the rules and you'll be rewarded," and then spend our lives expecting reality to run by them. When reality doesn't cooperate, we rarely update the rules. More often we complain that reality is cruel, retreat into ideology, and double down on beliefs that stopped working long ago.

These beliefs were once accurate maps. But the map is not the territory, and when the territory changes, the old map becomes waste paper, or even a lie. Most people's pain comes from this: they try to force the territory to fit their old map instead of drawing a new one on the spot. Mao called this "subjectivism" and "dogmatism," letting theory dominate practice and letting ego override ground truth. Two and a half millennia before Mao, Laozi said the same thing in the *Daodejing*, just in a different key. In Chapter 25, he writes:

> "Man follows Earth. Earth follows Heaven. Heaven follows the Tao. The Tao follows what is natural."

The Dao is the way things actually work, beneath the surface of human opinion and consensus. De, often mistranslated as "virtue," is closer to efficacy: aligning your actions with the Dao, moving with the grain of reality instead of forcing your way against it. Chapter 2 explains how:

> "Therefore the sage acts through non-action (wu wei) and teaches without words."

*Wu wei* is probably the most misunderstood concept in Chinese philosophy. People take it to mean lying flat, or doing nothing. What it means is acting without ego: not forcing things, not trying to impose your subjective will on a universe that doesn't care about you. Let go of ego and stop fighting reality, and reality stops fighting you, which is why the *Daodejing* says, "Through non-action, nothing is left undone." Conversely, the harder you try to force reality to submit to your will, the less it obeys. It deforms, it camouflages, it gives you the illusion that everything is under control, and then it quietly collapses underneath. Chapter 58 describes where forced control leads:

> "When the country is ruled with a light hand, the people are simple. When the country is ruled with severity, the people are cunning."

Manage with a microscope and what you get is cunning, because reality is an organism: when you try to "correct" it against its grain, it produces antibodies. This pattern is everywhere: the more a society stresses honesty, the more it signals a lack of trust; the more a company stresses its values, the more likely those values are missing. As Laozi noted in Chapter 18:

> "When the great Tao is forgotten, kindness and piety appear. When the body's intelligence declines, cleverness and knowledge appear... When the country is confused and chaotic, loyal ministers appear."

At this point you may sense a contradiction. Laozi says let go of ego, don't interfere, follow the Dao; Mao says understand reality so you can change it. I think they agree, and are simply talking about different scales. At the scale of planets and galaxies, human agency is negligible. You can't change the laws of thermodynamics, and Laozi's *wu wei* is the only answer: you can't bend the universe, so don't try. But in social systems such as organizations, companies, and relationships, you are inside the system as a participant, and what you understand and do is itself reshaping it. "Understanding reality" and "changing reality" become the same act. That's why dialectical materialism puts so much weight on practice: you test your ideas in reality, reality corrects you, you update your ideas, and you act again.

## No Investigation, No Right to Speak

All this may make ego sound like a bad thing, but I think it's only bad when it disconnects you from reality. An ego aligned with reality is a source of strength. Elon Musk's ego is enormous: "Humans must become multiplanetary," "Electric cars are the future." These are strong personal convictions he imposes on the world, and none of them can be derived from neutral facts. But his strategic ego rests on tactical first-principles reasoning.

When he decided to build rockets, he didn't accept the aerospace industry's consensus that rockets are just that expensive. He went straight to the ground truth: rockets are made of aluminum, titanium, copper, and carbon fiber, and those raw materials cost a tiny fraction of a rocket's price. Musk calls this the "Idiot Index," the ratio of a part's cost to the cost of its raw materials. If the ratio is too high, someone in the supply chain is being lazy or ripping you off. This habit of cutting through conventional thinking to physical reality runs through all of his work, and I think it is *wu wei* in practice: letting go of the industry's ego (consensus) and aligning with ground truth (materials, physics, and economics).

The same goes for Steve Jobs. His ego was legendary: his obsession with perfect design, his insistence that things "just work," his urge to control how hardware and software fit together. But none of these preferences were arbitrary. He studied calligraphy and was obsessed with how users behave; his aesthetic ego was built on watching how people interact with objects. Asked about his role after returning to Apple, he stressed collaboration over control; he wanted the company to keep a startup's agility while operating at scale. The core of Founder Mode, which Brian Chesky has talked about recently, is the same: presence, not absence. Jobs didn't micromanage. He took part, asked questions, and challenged assumptions. That is *wu wei* too, scaled up to a whole company: being present without imposing your will on the team.

Mao understood this early on. In his 1930 essay *Oppose Book Worship*, written long before he held power, he laid down what may be his most enduring maxim:

> "No investigation, no right to speak."

He went on: "To investigate a problem is, indeed, to solve it," and "Conclusions invariably come after investigation, and not before." No investigation, no right to speak, and this has nothing to do with moral authority. Without ground truth, your opinions are just noise.

## The Most Dangerous Illusion

But humans cannot escape narrative. As Yuval Noah Harari argues in *Sapiens*, humans rule the planet precisely because we can believe in shared fictions. Nations, corporations, money, and laws exist nowhere in physical reality. They are collective agreements, stories we tell ourselves. Thanks to these stories, we can coordinate hundreds of millions of people around abstract goals, building cities, going to the moon, creating the internet. But we also tend to mistake the story for reality, and we cling to stories long after they have stopped working.

The beliefs I listed earlier are stories of this kind, and they matched reality during the economic boom after World War II. Giving up narrative is impossible. What you can do is keep updating your narratives so they keep up with reality.

Reality and values are dialectical partners in an upward spiral. The cycle described in *On Practice* works this way: what separates a spiral from going in circles is that each turn takes your understanding one level higher. Say you believe that hard work leads to success, and reality slaps you in the face: you work hard but don't get promoted, and reality has negated your belief. You don't give up on yourself. You sublate the old belief, keeping the part that is true (effort matters) and discarding the part that is wrong (effort alone is enough), and you arrive at a higher understanding: "Strategic work in high-leverage areas leads to success." In *On Practice*, Mao wrote:

> "Practice, knowledge, again practice, and again knowledge. This form repeats itself in endless cycles, and with each cycle the content of practice and knowledge rises to a higher level."

The most dangerous illusion is "My values are correct, and reality just hasn't caught up," which is far more dangerous than "I don't have values." It keeps people stuck, clinging to their values as if they were sacred truths, as if letting go meant moral surrender. But values are just tools. When a hammer doesn't work, you don't pray for the nail to go soft. You switch to a drill.

Your beliefs are provisional, and when your model fails, it's the model that has to change. It is harsh to admit you were wrong, let go of ideas you've put your feelings into, and accept that the values you inherited may be obsolete.
