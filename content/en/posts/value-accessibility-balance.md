The deepest ideas are written in the best books, but you're scrolling TikTok. The most valuable conversations tend to make people anxious, while the most comfortable chats usually have little in them. Smart people run into the same paradox socially: you think you're providing value, but what the other person feels is a threat.

## Recognition Matters More Than Value

The philosopher Axel Honneth's recognition theory holds that a person's sense of self and psychological development depend on the recognition they get in their social relationships. Wanting to be recognized is a basic need for social animals like us, and it has little to do with vanity. So you can offer all the value and all the deep ideas you like, but if what the other person feels along the way is "I'm inferior," "I'm ignorant," or "I'm being crushed," they won't thank you. They'll avoid you.

Say you're chatting with someone, and before long you're talking about macroeconomics, philosophy, and historical trends. You feel you're sharing knowledge and providing value, but the other person is hearing two voices. Their rational brain says this is valuable and they should learn it; their emotional brain makes them aware of their own ignorance, and they feel anxious and uncomfortable. Most of the time the emotional brain wins, so they nod politely, find an excuse to leave, and don't reach out to you again. They needed recognition, and you gave them anxiety.

People say they want deep conversations, yet the most popular people in social settings are often the ones without much "depth" who simply make others comfortable, because getting value is painful. Deep reading takes a lot of brainpower, and it makes you face your ignorance and admit "I don't know," all of which makes people anxious. Scrolling short videos, or chatting with someone highly "agreeable," takes no brainpower and doesn't make you feel dumb; if anything, it makes you feel relaxed and accepted. Of course people want to improve, but that's human nature. You can't fight human nature. You can only understand it first and then play by its rules.

## Like Mixing Dough

I think socializing is a dynamic balance between value and accessibility. Value is how smart and insightful you are, and how many resources and abilities you have. Accessibility is how easy and effortless it is to be around you. Most smart people care only about value and ignore accessibility completely. They think, "I know so much, you should come and learn from me." But socializing isn't a classroom, and nobody is obliged to sit through your lecture. If your value is far above the other person's but your accessibility is low (talking to you is tiring, stressful, and makes them anxious), they won't think "what a steal." They'll think "I'm being crushed," and leave. Conversely, if your value is low but your accessibility is high (you're easygoing, warm, and supportive), people won't stick around for long either, because you have nothing to offer them. There's no fixed formula here. You adjust according to where you stand relative to the other person, the way you mix dough: too much flour, add water; too much water, add flour.

As for how to do it, my approach is to assess first and act second. First, be clear about what you want from the relationship. Do you want to be friends with this person, learn something from them, or get resources or opportunities? Or do you just think, "This person is impressive, I should know them"? If you can't answer, don't act yet. Vague goals lead to vague actions, and in the end you just waste time. Then work out where you stand in the relationship: whether your value is higher than theirs, lower, or about the same, what they need from you, and what you need from them. Be objective here, and neither sell yourself short nor puff yourself up.

Once you know where you stand, adjust your strategy. If your value is much higher than theirs, raise your accessibility and lower the threat they feel. Rein in the urge to preach, and shut up when you should. Don't rush to show how much you know; actually listen to what they're saying, and make them feel understood and recognized. That takes restraint, which is a different thing from "playing dumb." At the same time, watch out for freeloaders: if someone just comes to use up your time and attention without any real interaction, they're freeloading off you. If your value is much lower than theirs, you need to raise your value or offer something else (emotional support, resources, or opportunities). Otherwise you're the one freeloading, using up their time and giving nothing back. And if you're not really interested in the other person, you're better off giving up, because you can't fake real attention. People aren't stupid, and the sharp ones see through it at a glance.

The hardest part is controlling how much you put out. The biggest problem smart people have is often that they can't control the urge to say what they know. A topic comes up, and with a whole framework, a pile of cases, and a chain of reasoning in their heads, they can't resist pouring all of it out. That's really a kind of narcissism. Good communication means first judging how much the other person can take in, controlling the amount of information, leaving them room to think and respond, and shutting up completely when it's time to shut up, so that they feel they're contributing too, instead of just being lectured at.

## Accessibility Is Harder Than Value

Raising your value is relatively easy: reading, thinking, gaining experience, and learning skills are all things you can do through your own effort. Raising your accessibility, which is to say your "agreeableness," is much harder. Being agreeable isn't the same as being a doormat, saying yes to everything, or dropping your positions to please people. The agreeableness I mean is making the other person feel comfortable around you, understood and recognized, while you still hold on to your own boundaries and authenticity. That takes real attention. If you're just "holding it in" or "performing," they can feel it.

This is where many smart people fail socially. They're used to talking, not listening; used to judging, not understanding. What sets the really good ones apart is that they can say a great deal but know when not to. Smart people usually go through three stages: first they build up value, then they dare to show it, and finally they learn to hold it back.

You might find all this too calculating, hardly sincere, even manipulative. I don't see it that way. Not lying is already the most important moral line. Above that line, choosing when to speak, how much to say, and how to say it is a kind of wisdom. "Be your authentic self," "find your people," "don't shortchange yourself": these are feel-good platitudes. They sound lovely, but they don't tell you how to reach your goals. Socializing is an interaction, and if you only care about your own feelings and what you get to say, you're just amusing yourself. This assess-first, act-second approach is about reaching your goals. It doesn't guarantee that everyone will like you (that's impossible anyway), but it can help you build the relationships you want. If you want results, you have to play by the rules, and human nature is the most basic rule of all.
