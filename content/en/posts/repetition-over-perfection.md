We all love Nietzsche's line: "He who has a why can bear with any how." It sounds wise, and for life's big decisions (choosing a career, starting a company, committing to a relationship) it's probably true. A strong enough "why" really can carry you through almost any hardship. But most things worth doing don't come with a grand "why." Exercising, learning a skill, writing regularly, practicing an instrument, cooking good meals every day: none of these needs a philosophical justification, let alone a cosmic mission.

They are just simple behaviors that compound if you keep doing them. If you insist on waiting for a "why" strong enough to outweigh how hard the "how" is, you may never start at all. So I care more about something else: how to make repetition easy enough, so easy that it stops mattering whether it means anything.

Chasing perfection is exactly what gets in the way of repetition. Most people push in the wrong direction when they try to change a habit. They obsess over technique, optimal strategies, and perfect execution, reading books about the best workout routines and studying the ideal writing process. But if a behavior doesn't last, if it never makes it to the day it starts compounding, none of that matters. Charlie Munger called it "the fundamental algorithm of life: repeat what works." So find something that works and do it again and again; that's enough. There's no need to optimize every detail to perfection first.

Expecting one decision (a moment of clarity, or a burst of willpower) to change everything is lazy thinking, as if you could solve a problem once and never have to think about it again. Reality doesn't work that way. Nothing is done once and for all: stop going to the gym, and the muscle goes; stop writing, and your writing gets rusty. When we talk about focus, we usually mean keeping our attention on one thing right now. Repetition is focus in time: choosing the same thing every day, even long after it has stopped being new.

Duolingo became the most successful language-learning company in the world not by building the best curriculum, but by obsessing over retention. It has 70% of its employees working on product improvements and runs hundreds of A/B tests every week, almost all of them aimed at keeping users coming back. Over four years, its daily active users grew 4.5x and its current user retention rate improved by 21%. As their former head of product put it, they explicitly "prioritized retention over new user acquisition." More interesting still, academic research found that Duolingo's gamification sometimes makes users "too fixated" on streaks and leaderboards, distracting them from the learning itself. Yet the company kept doubling down on engagement, because they understood that someone who quits after three days will never learn a language.

Most good things in life take sustained effort. There are very few cases where you do something once and see results right away, and those are usually scams. Skills, relationships, wealth, health: all of them require you to stay in the game long enough for compounding to work, which is why imperfect repetition beats perfect execution every time. If the results depend on persistence, 90% of your effort should go into making sure you keep showing up. Whether each time is perfect comes second.

## Starting With a Resistance Band

The first hurdle in anything is starting. Stanford behavioral psychologist BJ Fogg has spent decades studying habit formation, and his conclusion is: Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt. When something is easy enough (high ability), you need very little motivation. That's why "just be more disciplined" keeps failing: the effort goes to the wrong place. Rather than straining to summon willpower, design your environment so the behavior becomes trivially easy. James Clear's "2-Minute Rule" in *Atomic Habits* makes the same point: the starting version of a habit should be so easy it takes less than two minutes. Of course you won't really do it for only two minutes, but starting is the only thing that matters.

That's how it went for me. I wanted to start strength training but couldn't stay consistent. Going to the gym felt like a big commitment: getting changed, getting there, the whole production. So I started with an elastic resistance band at home, and my rule was to use it during Zoom meetings. It worked because it took no extra time (I was sitting in meetings anyway), there was no friction (the band was right there), and there was no guilt (it didn't get in the way of anything else in my life). After a while, something shifted. I started to really feel my muscles getting stronger, I felt better, and then I wanted to do more. Eventually, I joined a gym. But if my starting goal had been "go to the gym three times a week," I never would have gotten there.

## Since I'm Already Here

Once you've started, you still have to keep going, and that's harder than starting. Luckily, psychology can help here, with the "might as well" effect. After I started going to the gym, I picked up another little trick: each time, I tell myself I'm only going for 30 minutes. Just 30 minutes, which barely disrupts anything. "How much could it hurt?" I think. But once I'm there, I never leave after 30 minutes. I've already done the hardest part (getting there), I've changed into gym clothes, I've warmed up, and at that point the cost of staying a little longer is tiny. The "I'm already here anyway" feeling kicks in. I never intended to stay only 30 minutes, but I needed to believe I could, to get myself to the gym in the first place.

This is the sunk cost fallacy turned around. Normally, sunk cost is a cognitive trap: you keep doing something only because you've already put something into it, even when it no longer makes sense. But when you're building a habit, sunk cost works for you. Behavioral economists call arrangements like this commitment devices: deliberately putting something in up front to bind your future self. The membership fee is money, changing into workout clothes is effort, driving over is time and distance, and walking in to start warming up is a psychological investment. Each layer makes quitting a little harder and continuing a little easier. At that point you hardly need willpower, because the cost-benefit math has changed: you've already paid the startup cost, so you might as well finish. That's why showing up is 90% of the battle. Once you're there, inertia is on your side.

Some habits are also just easier to keep than others, and the difference is in how they're built. Some add something good to your life; those are incremental. Others take something away from your life, or ask you to deny yourself; those are restrictive. Compare building muscle with losing weight by dieting. Building muscle is incremental: you lift, you see your body change, you feel stronger, every session brings progress, missing a day doesn't erase what you've gained, and the feedback comes from you, quickly. Losing weight by dieting is restrictive: you turn down food you want, you fight hunger and cravings all the time, progress is slow and easily reversed, one "cheat day" can undo a week of effort, and you're constantly at war with your own desires.

Psychologically, the two are far apart. An incremental habit goes along with your instincts and steers the desire somewhere useful, so you don't have to fight yourself. A restrictive habit keeps you in conflict with yourself. It also comes with a trap: when you finally reach a goal by holding back, your first instinct is often to reward yourself. You save up $100, so you buy a $900 jacket to celebrate. You lose 5 pounds, so you treat yourself to a big meal. The reward wipes out the progress. That's also why behavior change research tends to find approach motivation more effective than avoidance motivation: moving toward something good is easier than moving away from something bad. James Clear writes: "Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you wish to become." By that logic, an incremental habit is a vote for something, while a restrictive habit forces you to vote against yourself.

## Showing Up Beats Talent

This brings me to a conclusion that sounds a little unfair: showing up consistently matters more than talent. Someone of average ability who shows up every day will beat someone with exceptional talent who shows up now and then, every single time. Compound interest doesn't ask whether you're smart. It asks whether you're there. Say one person invests $100 every month for 30 years; in the end they will be far ahead of the genius who invests $10,000 once and then disappears. Skills, relationships, and reputation work the same way: if you're not there, nothing compounds.

Showing up sounds like the most basic thing. But look around: among the people you know, probably not many have gone to the gym consistently for a year, written something every week for a year, or practiced a skill deliberately for a full year. So the bar for doing better than most people is shockingly low (and it has little to do with laziness; showing up consistently is just hard). Phillippa Lally's research at University College London found that it takes a median of 66 days for a behavior to become automatic, not the mythical 21 days. And it varies a lot, anywhere from 18 to 254 days, depending on the person and on how complex the habit is. In other words, the first two months are going to be rough. Every single time, you have to consciously decide to do the thing. It feels effortful and not at all natural. This is when most people quit, long before compounding kicks in.

From the outside, you can't really tell who has talent and who has simply kept at it longer. Someone who writes a mediocre essay every week for three years looks more impressive than someone who writes one brilliant essay and then vanishes, because by the third year, the one who kept writing isn't mediocre anymore. Repetition alone has made them better, and they have a whole body of work that shows they're serious. The person with talent who doesn't keep at it has nothing to show.

The good news is that showing up is something you control, which makes it the thing most worth working on. Talent is a genetic lottery; intelligence, creativity, and athleticism are handed out unevenly and unfairly. But showing up is an engineering problem, and the resistance band, the "might as well" effect, and incremental habits are all ways of solving it. You can't will yourself to be twice as smart, but you can absolutely design your environment to double your consistency.

If persistence matters more than quality, and if engagement mechanics work even when they distract from the "real" goal, then you arrive at a slightly awkward conclusion: the thing that keeps you going doesn't have to be the thing that makes you good. Your fitness tracker counting steps doesn't make you healthier, but if it gets you to walk more, it works. Duolingo's gamification won't teach you grammar, but if it gets you to practice every day, you'll learn anyway. Writing is the same. What you write for engagement may not be your best thinking, and people who care about craft and quality will feel that something is off, as if we were optimizing for the wrong thing. In a sense, we are. But once you quit, quality is out of the question, and as long as it keeps you writing, you'll eventually develop your own voice. The math is simple: perfection for 0 days is still 0, while mediocrity for 1000 days is mastery.

Back to Nietzsche. He wasn't wrong. A strong "why" really does help: if you're building a company, fighting for a cause, or pursuing something that takes years of sacrifice, you need a reason strong enough to keep you going. But for most of what matters in daily life, the habits that slowly compound into health, skills, and personal growth, you don't need a why. You need a how that's easy enough not to require one. The irony is that the "why" often shows up after you start. Nobody falls in love with writing by working out why writing matters. You fall in love with it by writing badly for six months and discovering that you've gotten better.
