Every time there's a major technological breakthrough, people assume working hours will get shorter. After the washing machine was invented, housework should have shrunk; after the internet spread, finding information should have gotten faster; after AI arrived, repetitive work should have disappeared. Yet we're busier than in any other era, and it has been this way after every round of new technology. Most people blame capitalist greed, or blame themselves for not being good enough. I think both explanations are too shallow. We think we're choosing technology, but in fact technology is choosing us.

In the 19th century, the economist William Stanley Jevons observed something counterintuitive: as steam engines became more efficient and the cost of using coal fell, coal consumption went up instead of down. The reason was simple. Lower costs made many tasks that weren't worth doing before suddenly worth doing. This is the famous Jevons Paradox: efficiency gains expand the uses of a resource, and total consumption ends up rising.

Washing machines, the internet and AI all work the same way. The washing machine made laundry easier, and society's standard for "clean" rose with it: what used to count as clean no longer did, and people washed their clothes more and more often. A report used to take three books. Now it takes fifty web pages, ten papers and three data sources. Search did get faster; the time it saved all went into the extra material. With AI, a blog post used to be enough; now it also needs images, video, SEO, A/B-tested headlines and conversion tracking.

The Jevons Paradox explains why the time we save gets eaten up, but not why standards keep rising. One reason is competition. Once everyone has a washing machine, being clean is the bare minimum and hardly counts as an advantage; once everyone can use search engines and AI, having more information or working faster is the same. Technology turns advantages into table stakes, so everyone has to keep raising the ante, in an arms race with no finish line. Audit costs are rising too. The more advanced the technology, the more complex the system and the more serious the consequences of a mistake, and so the higher the cost of review, compliance, explanation and accountability. AI generates content quickly, but you have to spend time checking it for hallucinations, for whether it fits the brand, and for whether it might stir up controversy.

## Where the Gains Go

If that were all, the problem wouldn't be this serious. What I think really makes people busier and busier, with no way back, are a few default settings of capitalism. Capitalism's logic is to reinvest profits, so the gains from efficiency go by default into the next round of growth and rarely turn into time off. After Amazon used AI to optimize its warehouses and logistics, the savings went into delivering faster: from two-day shipping to next-day, then same-day, and even two-hour delivery. Each efficiency gain became a new service standard, and employees didn't end up working any less.

Platforms like Salesforce use AI to help salespeople write emails automatically, summarize calls and make predictions, and by that logic each rep should manage more accounts and cover more territory: "since you're more efficient now, you should close more deals." Netflix used its recommendation algorithm to improve retention, and the acquisition costs it saved went into more original content and more countries. They became neither profit nor time off.

Then there's the superstar effect. Once technology brings marginal costs down, economies of scale and network effects concentrate more at the top. On YouTube and Instagram, individual creators use AI tools for automatic editing, AI voiceovers, subtitles and translation into other languages, so one person can match the output that used to take a small team, running English, Spanish and Japanese channels at once and posting several videos a day. Even so, mid-tier creators barely get by, because algorithmic recommendation follows a power law: the top creators, who have the resources for expensive content, take 80% of the traffic and leave scraps for everyone else.

It's the same for programmers. A 10x engineer with Cursor and Copilot can do the work of a small team. That doesn't make life easier for other programmers: hiring drops, and more is expected of the ones who remain, who now have to write prompts, review AI output and iterate quickly in ten directions at once on top of writing code. In consulting and design, independent advisors who use AI can take on more projects at the same time, squeezing mid-sized agencies. Either you're a top expert who uses AI to multiply your capacity tenfold, or you're cheap labor that AI replaces, and the middle is disappearing. AI reinforces winner-take-all rules, and technology widens both the wealth gap and the anxiety gap.

Measurement is another default. Technology makes everything measurable, so blank time loses its legitimacy: being idle looks like slacking, and "good enough" becomes "not enough yet." Call centers used to track resolution rates and average response times. Now AI can analyze the sentiment, word choice, pause length and expressions of empathy in each conversation, so even empathy gets quantified. Agents have to solve the problem and also show, in every sentence, a "warmth" the algorithm can detect.

For programmers, GitHub Copilot acceptance rate, code review pass rate, PR response time and deployment frequency are all visible. You used to be able to say "I'm thinking about the architecture"; now you have to prove that the thinking produced quantifiable value. Thinking becomes suspicious blank time that has to be defended with output. Content is the same. Beyond views, there's dwell time, scroll depth, share rate and conversion rate, and every paragraph, headline and image you make comes with data telling you "readers dropped 30% here." Data can always find something to improve, so optimization never ends.

Put together, these turn efficiency gains into work intensity. Capitalists really are greedy, but the bigger reason is that the system's default settings work this way. As long as capital decides how the gains are divided, as long as competition is about relative ranking with no line where enough is enough, and as long as management is built on measurement, efficiency gains will turn into more work.

## When Brainwork Gets Cheap

Every technological revolution makes people busier, but this round of AI has something special about it. Past technologies mainly lowered the cost of physical labor and information processing. Washing machines replaced washing by hand and search engines replaced libraries, but the marginal cost of brainwork stayed high: writing an article, making a design or writing a piece of code still took a lot of human time.

AI is the first technology to push the marginal cost of brainwork close to zero, so the long-tail tasks that used to be dropped as not worth doing are now all worth doing. Personalized emails used to go only to VIP clients; now every user can get one. Content used to be made only in English; now it can be made in twenty languages. You used to optimize only the main flow; now every edge case can be optimized. The amount of work grows exponentially.

Our role has changed too. You used to be the one who executed; now you orchestrate, review, and carry the responsibility. You no longer write the code yourself, but you have to check the code AI writes for bugs, for whether it fits the architecture and for whether it adds technical debt, and the same goes for copy. The more automation there is, the heavier the responsibility at the last gate: AI does 95% of the work, but the remaining 5% requires you to bear 100% of the responsibility.

AI also compresses the rhythm. Information used to travel with a delay, and waiting was legitimate. Now AI can respond 24/7, so organizations tighten their SLAs and speed up release cycles. Waiting time gets deleted, the buffer disappears, and people get locked into a feedback loop that never stops.

Not every technology makes people busier, of course. Some really did create leisure (refrigerators, for example, cut down the number of shopping trips people made each day). Whether a technology turns its gains into work or into rest depends on a few things: whether the costs it lowers sit at core bottlenecks such as brainwork, communication and distribution; how many tasks it turns from not worth doing into worth doing; whether people are judged by relative ranking or against a fixed line; whether the gains by default get reinvested in growth or used to buy time; and whether it shortens feedback cycles. When all of these hold at once, people will almost certainly get busier, and AI meets every one of them.

## Wheat Domesticated Us

Now we can go back to the question from the beginning: why efficiency gains always turn into work intensity. The usual explanations are capitalist exploitation, competitive pressure and social comparison. All true, but I don't think they go deep enough. The deeper reason is that technology selects what kind of humans survive. We think we're the ones choosing technology, the washing machine over washing by hand, search engines over the library, AI over doing it ourselves, but in fact technology is choosing us.

People who used washing machines could keep higher standards of cleanliness and had an edge socially and at work; people who used search engines got information faster and made better decisions; people who use AI can cover more product lines and serve more customers. Those who didn't adopt them slowly fell behind. Technology changes the conditions for being selected. When a new technology appears, the earliest adopters get a temporary advantage, but that advantage soon becomes table stakes, the standard resets, and those who can't keep up are eliminated. The system only looks at whether you meet the new standard; whether you want to rest is none of its concern.

This is domestication. When we domesticated wheat, we thought we were mastering nature, but Yuval Noah Harari points out in *Sapiens* that it was really wheat that domesticated humans. Growing wheat made people settle down, work longer hours and live under more stress, but it could feed a larger population, so the groups that grew wheat won out and the groups that didn't disappeared. People became busier and more tired, but they had no choice, because the conditions for being selected had changed.

Technology works the same way. People who use AI produce faster and can cover more scenarios and take on more tasks, while those who don't slowly lose their competitiveness. The system rewards people who grind harder and weeds out people who want to rest. What any one person prefers doesn't matter; the direction we evolve in as a group is set by the selection pressure that technology creates.

I'm not preaching fatalism. History does have examples of efficiency gains being turned into leisure, such as labor movements, the eight-hour workday and paid vacation, but all of them took collective coordination and institutional commitment. Without that kind of commitment, the default outcome is that technology domesticates people.

So I think technology amplifies the commitments you already have, but it won't make a new one for you. If your commitment is growth first, proving your value through output, technology will amplify it and make you faster and push you harder. If your commitment is leisure first, proving your value through time, technology will amplify that too and give you more room to rest, think and create. But in reality, most people have never actively made this commitment. It's a default, implicit, set for them by the system, and the system's default settings are that gains get reinvested in growth, competition decides value, and everything is managed through measurement. Technology just amplifies those defaults.

Whether AI makes you busier or gives you more leisure depends on what you commit your new productivity to in advance: more features, markets and output, or more blank time, thinking and leisure. Technology won't make this choice for you. It will just amplify your choice a hundredfold and then ask you: is this really what you want?
