We live in an era of over-explanation. Open any social media app and you'll find someone explaining exactly why you're unhappy, why your relationships fail, why you can't focus, why you're anxious. There's a theory for everything: attachment styles, love languages, growth mindset, impostor syndrome. They all promise control. Learn the framework, identify your pattern, adjust the variables, and the problem is solved. I call this the magic button worldview. I don't think knowing more psychological theories makes you happier. I think it makes you more anxious, which makes most of this attribution worse than useless.

## You Can't A/B Test a Marriage

Let me start with a basic standard for attribution: it only makes sense when you can run experiments. In physics or chemistry you can isolate variables, repeat the experiment and say with confidence that A causes B, which is why our bridges don't collapse and our drugs work. Life is different. Relationships, careers and historical events happen once. You can't rerun your childhood with different parents, A/B test your marriage, or replay 2020 with a different pandemic response to see what would have happened.

Yet we treat these one-time events like lab experiments. "My relationship failed because of attachment issues." "I'm not successful because of impostor syndrome." "The company failed because of poor product-market fit." Maybe. Or maybe there were a million other factors you couldn't see. Nassim Taleb makes this point in *Antifragile*: medicine and social science try to explain complex systems with statistical methods, but complex systems are non-linear and path-dependent. You can't isolate the variable, and you can't even see everything that matters.

Most of the time we see only the tip of the iceberg and attribute as if we could see the whole ocean. A farmer in ancient China might blame the local magistrate for high taxes, unaware that the emperor had issued a decree for the entire empire. A retail investor thinks they picked the winning stocks, not realizing the whole market was in a bull run. An employee blames their burnout on personal weakness and doesn't see that the organization itself is dysfunctional.

The magic button illusion usually works like this. You run into a problem (relationship stress, career confusion, general unhappiness) and find a theory that "explains" it, say attachment theory or impostor syndrome. You feel relief ("Oh, that's why I'm like this!"), and then you believe you have control: if I just adjust X, I can fix Y. It's as if you had found a volume knob: turn it left and the problem shrinks, turn it right and the solution grows.

Life doesn't work like that. Even if you correctly identified one cause (already nearly impossible, given how limited our minds are), you still couldn't control it. Most factors are outside your control: the economy crashes, your partner has their own psychology, your boss has different incentives, and a lot of it is plain randomness. The system is also too complex, with a hundred thousand variables interacting, so changing one often sets off effects you didn't expect. And your past constrains your present. You can't undo attachment patterns formed in childhood by reading a blog post.

The cruel irony is that the more you believe you should be able to control something, the more anxious you get when you can't. Before you learned attachment theory, a relationship that wasn't working was just that: sad, but simple. After you learned it, the same relationship became a project you were failing at: "I'm anxious-attached, which explains my behavior, which means I should be able to change it, but I can't seem to change it, which means I'm failing, which means I'm broken, which means every future relationship will fail too..."

## Contagion Over Validity

Taleb has an explanation for why theories like these spread so widely even when they don't help: "It is contagion that determines the fate of a theory in social science, not its validity." They spread because they tell people what they want to hear: you can understand yourself, you can control your life, you are not helpless. The attention market runs on this. Information spreads when it makes you feel understood, when it's simple enough to repeat, when knowing it makes you look sophisticated, and when it gives you something to do about your problems. Whether it's actually true is not on that list.

The pop psychology industry lives off this. Attachment theory gets simplified into neat boxes, love languages turn into a personality test, and growth mindset becomes a corporate buzzword. Every simplification loses some nuance and makes the idea easier to pass on. You, the consumer, pick up these frameworks because everyone else uses them and you crave the relief they promise; very few of us have ever tested one with a rigorous experiment. It's mimetic desire applied to epistemology, a kind of mass delusion: you want to know because others know, and you want the control they claim to have.

## Where Words Don't Reach

A deeper problem is that some things can't be put into words. Wittgenstein wrote, "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." But we don't stay silent. We try to explain everything with language and logic, including what it means to love someone, what makes a life meaningful, or why you're drawn to some people and not others. You can gesture toward these with metaphors, but however articulate you are, the thing itself stays out of reach, because language has boundaries.

A lot of intelligence lives outside language. AI doesn't really think in language: despite the name "large language model," these models operate on tokens, and language is just the interface they use to talk to us. Their "thinking" (if we can call it that) happens in high-dimensional vector spaces that don't map cleanly to human words. Animals are the same. A zebra foal can stand within 20 minutes of birth and run shortly after, and nobody taught it; the knowledge is encoded in its DNA, compiled over millions of years of evolution. Humans have this too. Musicians improvise without putting their choices into words, athletes make split-second decisions that bypass language, and parents love their children for reasons they can't explain.

But we've been over-educated into believing that if you can't articulate something, you don't really understand it. So we rush to force what can't be said into linguistic boxes, learning theories and adopting frameworks to explain ourselves to ourselves, and we lose the ability to simply experience something without analyzing it. Education itself is valuable. Over-education is when you've learned so many theories that you no longer trust your direct experience.

You meet someone and feel a connection, and instead of enjoying it you start theorizing: is this novelty, hormones, projection, will it fade? You've learned so many theories about why connections happen that you can't just accept that they do. Or you have a good day at work, and instead of feeling accomplished you wonder whether it's sustainable, whether you're setting unrealistic standards, whether it's just Dunning-Kruger. This is what I mean when I say that letting learned theories prevent you from being happy is a form of over-education.

The *Tao Te Ching* opens with "The Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao," which points to the same thing: some truths can't be written into a manual, and some wisdom only comes through practice and presence. Learning theory has a concept called "unlearning," and I think it's as important as learning. You need to unlearn the urge to explain and control everything, and the belief that more knowledge means more happiness. Most of the time you can only change your interpretation of things, not the things themselves. That's a hard pill to swallow in a culture obsessed with agency and optimization, but I believe it's true.

What I'd suggest instead is to watch and feel what is actually happening, and to accept that most things are beyond your control and your understanding, and that this is okay. That doesn't make psychological theories worthless. Some are useful as tools for observation: Maslow's hierarchy of needs can help you recognize patterns, and knowing about cognitive biases can make you humbler about your own thinking.

The difference is whether you use a theory to observe or to control. Used to observe, it sounds like "Interesting, I notice I tend to seek approval. Let me sit with that." Used to control, it sounds like "I have anxious attachment, so I need to do X, Y and Z to fix myself or I'm doomed." The first comes from curiosity and leaves room for complexity and mystery; the second comes from anxiety and demands certainty and control. So the next time a psychological framework claims to explain you, ask yourself whether it helps you observe more clearly or just gives you another magic button to obsess over. Most of the time, I think, it's the latter.

I know this might sound like nihilism, or like giving up: "Nothing can be explained, nothing can be controlled, so what's the point?" I see it as realism. The point is to live more freely, to loosen the tyranny of "should," and to stop treating yourself as a broken machine that needs the right manual, or your life as a problem that better theories will solve. You are a complex being in a complex world, and most of what happens is emergent, unpredictable and mysterious.

Freedom comes from accepting this and letting go of the fantasy that if you read one more book or learned one more framework, you'd finally have it all figured out. You won't. None of us will. But you can still observe, feel and act, without the crushing weight of thinking you should be able to control it all.
